# What Nobody Tells You About Color Theory References

I used to think color theory references was for control freaks. Then I lost a $15k project because I couldn't find the right reference in time.

The client wanted "something like that orange from the Thompson project." You know the one—that weird coral-salmon hybrid that looked terrible in isolation but perfect with their branding.

I knew I'd saved it. Somewhere. But somewhere isn't good enough when the client is waiting.

Twenty minutes of scrolling. Forty minutes of searching folders. An hour of growing panic. I never found it. We went with a safe blue instead. The client was fine with it. But I knew—we could have had the perfect reference if I'd just been able to find it.

That's when I started taking color reference management seriously.

## Why Most Advice Fails

Search for "color theory references" and you'll find the same advice everywhere. Create folders. Use tags. Be consistent.

Boring. And more importantly, wrong.

Those systems assume you have infinite time and motivation. You don't. You're a working designer with deadlines, difficult clients, and a brain that works in mysterious ways.

**The problem:** Color is visual. You don't think in hex codes or Pantone numbers. You think in "that warm red from the Italian restaurant branding" or "the muted green from that nature documentary."

Traditional organization systems force you to translate visual memory into text. That's where they break down.

## What Your Brain Actually Needs

Here's the thing about human memory: it's visual and contextual.

You don't remember "that reference in the Minimal folder tagged with blue." You remember "that thing I saved while working on the Johnson project, the one with the weird gradient."

Your organization system should match that.

**The Three Rules That Actually Work:**

### 1. If You Can't See It, It Doesn't Exist

Out of sight, out of mind. If your references are buried in folders, you'll forget they're there.

Visual organization isn't pretty, it's functional. When you can see your references, you remember you have them. Simple as that.

This is why Mare uses a grid layout by default. Everything visible. No digging.

### 2. Save For Projects, Not For Someday

That folder called "Inspiration"? It's a graveyard. Nothing good happens there.

Save references for specific projects. Even if that project is "personal exploration March 2026." Give it a container. A reason to exist.

In Mare, every reference lives in a project. No orphan images floating around. When you need that coral-salmon, you know exactly which project to check.

### 3. Done Is Better Than Perfect

A messy system you use beats a perfect system you abandoned. Every time.

Don't let the pursuit of the ideal setup stop you from having a working setup.

Mare embraces this. Quick capture first. Organize later (if you want to). The important thing is getting the reference saved while you're thinking about it.
